The Name-Letter Effect: Are You Attracted to Your Own Initials?

August 30, 2026 • 4 Min Read

Have you ever noticed that a surprising number of Dentists are named Dennis? Or that a disproportionate number of people named Louis live in St. Louis? This isn't just a bizarre coincidence. It is an extensively documented psychological bias known as the Name-Letter Effect.

First identified by Belgian psychologist Jozef Nuttin in 1985, the Name-Letter Effect states that humans have a profound, subconscious preference for the letters in their own name—especially their initials. Because we associate our names with our identity, our brains automatically trigger positive feelings of familiarity and safety when we see those letters elsewhere.

How It Controls Your Life Choices

This psychological phenomenon is an aspect of implicit egotism—the idea that people gravitate toward things that resemble themselves. Its effects ripple out into major life decisions.

Research published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology found stunning correlations. People are statistically more likely to:

  • Choose careers that sound like their names (Dennis = Dentist, Lauren = Lawyer).
  • Live in cities starting with their initials (Phil lives in Philadelphia, Virginia moves to Virginia).
  • Donate to hurricane relief efforts if the hurricane shares their initial (e.g., people named Katie donating heavily after Hurricane Katrina).
  • Buy products from brands that match their initials (Peter drinks Pepsi, Carl drinks Coca-Cola).

The Name-Letter Effect in Romance

Perhaps most fascinating is how this affects our love lives. Studies have shown that you are statistically significantly more likely to marry someone whose first or last name shares letters with your own name. If your name is Eric, you are drawn to an Erica. If your name is Chloe, you might find a Charlie exceptionally charming.
